# Text on projected pages: how much a page holds, and how its lines are set

**Note of 2026-11-16.** This page studies pages of text shown in a room. The rulings its "What follows" reports, R2
and R3 with a capacity counted in lines of a typed tier, describe pages of statements and were set aside that day with
the second proof, after the owner's words of 08:06:21 and 08:06:24: 「产品定位应该是架构图，以图为主，不是堆砌文字吧？」「文字的部分只是辅助？」 (glosses:
"the product's positioning should be architecture figures, figures first, not text heaped up, surely?"; "the text part
only assists?"). The typed tiers were withdrawn the same day. The page stands as a record of what was read and
calculated.

A research page. It records how much text a page holds when its letters are large enough for a room, how much text
pages shown to an audience carry in practice, what experiments on narrated talks measured, and what the published
requirements say about setting lines of Chinese text. It gives numbers and practice and decides nothing. It is the
second half of one study; the first half, [letter height by viewing distance](viewing-distance-and-letter-height.md),
holds the floors and the arithmetic that the capacity table below starts from.

## Question

When letters reach the floors that a room asks for, how many characters does a page hold; how many words do pages
shown to an audience carry, and on what evidence; is a page of much text, read in silence by a room, a practice
anyone has measured; and how long and how far apart are lines of Chinese text set? The owner's sentences that raised
the question are quoted in the [committee's record](edition-forms-committee-2026-11-15.md).

## Short answer

- By arithmetic a full page at 4 H holds 1,130–1,320 Chinese characters at 23 pt, 400–430 at 40 pt; one page had 3,272.
- Pages that accompany a speaker are sparser: 21 words a slide against 41.5; a median of 40; at most 30 at one meeting.
- The measured gains (recall 79 % against 69 %; d = 0.81; 16 of 16 tests) are all for narrated talks.
- Much text read in silence is another tradition (six-page memo, handout, slidedoc), read on paper or at a desk.
- One study of projected Chinese: 24′ and 48′ beat 16′ in proofreading. No source measures a dense page read by a room.

## Findings

### The study's own summary

- **What a page then holds** (arithmetic: a 1440 × 740 pt block filled edge to edge, line pitch 1.5–1.75 em): about
  1,130–1,320 Chinese characters at 23 pt, 750–850 at 28.5 pt, 400–430 at 40 pt; at 6 H about 500–590, 330–360 and
  170–190. Page 28 of the sample book carried 3,272.
- **Projected pages that accompany a speaker are sparser than that.** Counted or prescribed: 21 words a slide in the
  assertion-evidence condition against 41.5, a median of 40 words in Tufte's sample, at most 30 words and 6 lines at
  one IEEE conference. The measured gains (recall 79 % against 69 %; d = 0.81; 16 of 16 tests) are all for narrated
  talks.
- **Much text read in silence is the other tradition** (Amazon's six-page memo, Tufte's handout, the "slidedoc"), and
  it is read on paper or at a desk. One study of Chinese text read from a projection was found: 24′ and 48′ gave
  better proofreading than 16′. No source found measures a dense page read silently by a room.

### Practice for projected text

**Font-size minimums.**

- IEEE International Electron Devices Meeting, speaker rules: no font under 24 point; text-only pages no more than
  30 words and 6 lines; a 16:9 page 13.33 in × 7.5 in. **[fetch]** <https://ieee-iedm.org/speakers/presentation-info/>.
  By arithmetic, 24 pt on a page 540 pt high is 4.44 % of the height, 40 pt on the 900 pt page; its capitals reach
  16′ at 6.7 H and 20′ at 5.3 H, a Chinese character 20′ at 7.0 H.
- University of Waterloo, Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, presentation guidelines, "5.2.3 Font
  Size": slide titles 32 pt, main points 24 pt, secondary 20 pt, tertiary 18 pt. No reasoning from distance.
  **[fetch]** <https://ece.uwaterloo.ca/~dwharder/Presentations/Guidelines/VisualAids/TextFormat/Size/>
- Reported and not opened: IEEE NMDC 2021 (24 point, 30 words, six lines), IEEE EDUCON 2024 (18 point), Asian
  Population Association 2026 (24 pt, 28–32 preferred). **[snippet]** By arithmetic, 18 pt on a 540 pt page is
  30 pt on the 900 pt page and reaches 16′ for capitals at 5.0 H.
- All of these are rules of meetings and departments. None cites a study.

**Assertion-evidence slides.**

- Alley, Schreiber, Ramsdell and Muffo, "How the Design of Headlines in Presentation Slides Affects Audience
  Retention", Technical Communication 53(2): 225–234, 2006. **[text]**
  <http://writing.engr.psu.edu/alley_et_al_2006.pdf>. Four sections of one geoscience course (200, 202, 201 and 136
  students), same instructor; two taught from slides with phrase headlines, two from slides with sentence headlines
  of at most two lines. Measured: exam questions. On 15 questions whose answer stood in a sentence headline, 79 %
  correct against 69 % (chi-square, .001 level); 7 of the 15 significantly higher, 2 lower. On 17 questions whose
  answer stood in the body of both designs, 74 % against 73 %, no difference. The headline was 28 pt bold against
  24 pt body, and the authors name that emphasis as one cause.
- Garner and Alley, "How the Design of Presentation Slides Affects Audience Comprehension: A Case for the
  Assertion–Evidence Approach", International Journal of Engineering Education 29(6): 1564–1579, 2013. **[text]**
  <https://www.ijee.ie/articles/Vol29-6/23_ijee2791ns.pdf>. 110 engineering students watched one narrated talk on
  magnetic resonance imaging, the same 1,000-word script over eleven slides in either design. Mean words per slide:
  21.2 (assertion-evidence, every slide with a graphic) and 41.5 (common practice, 64 % with a graphic). Essay
  comprehension favoured assertion-evidence, t(93.43) = 3.67, p < 0.001, d = 0.81; fewer misconceptions,
  F(1,108) = 9.84, d = 0.47; lower rated mental effort, d = −0.50; and a surprise test a week later, t(93) = 4.34,
  d = 0.89.
- What these are evidence for: a narrated talk, students, one topic each. The designs differ in several things at
  once (sentence headline, graphics, fewer words, builds), so the amount of text is not isolated. They are not
  evidence about a page read in silence.

**Reading and listening at once.**

- Mayer and Fiorella, "Principles for Reducing Extraneous Processing in Multimedia Learning", chapter 12 of The
  Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning, 2nd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2014 (year taken from the file's
  metadata and DOI 10.1017/CBO9781139547369.015). **[text]**. Redundancy principle: people learn more deeply from
  graphics and narration than from graphics, narration and on-screen text; supported in 16 of 16 tests, median
  effect size d = 0.86, on transfer tests. The chapter limits it to narration and on-screen text that are
  identical.
- Mayer, "Applying the Science of Learning", American Psychologist, 2008: redundancy 5 of 5 tests, median 0.72.
  Mayer and Johnson, "Revising the Redundancy Principle in Multimedia Learning", Journal of Educational Psychology
  100(2): 380–386, 2008: two or three words placed beside the part of the graphic they name help retention at no
  cost to transfer. Kalyuga, Chandler and Sweller, "When Redundant On-Screen Text in Multimedia Technical
  Instruction Can Interfere With Learning", Human Factors 46(3): 567–581, 2004: three experiments with technical
  trainees; the same text shown while it is spoken lowered test scores, and showing it after the narration did
  better. All three **[snippet]**.
- What these are evidence for: a speaker saying what the screen shows in the same words. They say nothing about a
  page that no one reads aloud.

**Critiques and the opposite answers.**

- Tufte, The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint (Graphics Press, 2003; publisher and year from memory). **[fetch]**, from
  an uncorrected transcription, not the print pamphlet: <https://www.readkong.com/page/the-cognitive-style-of-powerpoint-1734467>.
  Counted data: median words on text-only slides 97 (26 slides in three Boeing reports on Columbia), 40 (1,460
  slides in 189 reports found online, March 2003), 15 (654 slides in 28 textbooks). Characters per page: 250 for
  those reports, 98 for the textbooks, 630 for the Boeing slides, against 2,500 to 13,600 for a page of the
  best-selling books he counted and 2,400 to 4,100 for a screen of news sites. Argument, not data: that the format
  weakens reasoning; that printed handouts should largely replace slides (a sheet of 11 × 17 in folded in half, up
  to 60,000 characters).
- Amazon, 2017 Letter to Shareholders (Jeffrey P. Bezos, published 18 April 2018): the company does not do
  slide presentations; it writes narratively structured six-page memos and reads one in silence at the start of each
  meeting. **[fetch]** <https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2017-letter-to-shareholders>. The 2004 email
  that began the practice was seen only as reported. **[snippet]** This is a company's account of its practice: a
  document read at reading distance, and no measurement.
- Duarte, "Slidedocs" (2014): a visual document made in presentation software, to be read and referred to and not
  projected; her firm's blog is reported to put it at 150–200 words a page against 40 or fewer on a projected
  slide. **[snippet]** No primary source was found for consulting-style dense pages; what was seen is opinion.

### Chinese text on screens

**W3C, Requirements for Chinese Text Layout** (clreq). **[fetch]** <https://www.w3.org/TR/clreq/>. The clauses read,
with those on mixed text:

- 6.2.2.1: body text should in principle be justified; the last line of a paragraph need not reach the line end.
  5.6.1: the line gap must be consistent, not smaller than half the font size for single-sided setting and not
  smaller than 5/8 for double-sided. 7.1.1.4: the line length is specified as a number of characters; no figure. No
  number for the usual line gap or for body size.
- Mixed text, 6.3.3: spacing of up to 1/4 em between a Chinese character and a Western letter or numeral, none at
  line start or end, none beside Chinese pause or stop marks; a Western word space is the other approach.
  6.2.2.3: in compression that spacing should stay at least 1/8 em. 6.2.2.4: in expansion up to 1/2 em, many styles
  only 1/3 em. Nothing was found there on matching a Western face's baseline or x-height to the Chinese face.

**W3C, Requirements for Japanese Text Layout** (JLReq), section 2.4.2, on the base page design (kihon-hanmen).
**[fetch]** <https://www.w3.org/TR/jlreq/>

- Size: 9 point is common for body text and 8 point the minimum, in print.
- Line length: a multiple of the character size; at most about 52 characters in vertical setting and about 40 in
  horizontal; if the page would take longer lines, consider several columns. The worked examples use a column gap of
  two characters.
- Line gap: the same throughout; usually between half an em and one em; half an em where lines are short, one em or
  close to it where a line is longer than 35 characters; no need to exceed one em, which would lower legibility.
  For comparison it gives one third of an em as standard in Western setting. So the line pitch is 1.5 to 2.0 em.
- What transfers: Chinese body text is set solid in square frames like Japanese. What may not: the figures describe
  printed books read at arm's length.

**WCAG 2.2, success criterion 1.4.8 Visual Presentation (level AAA)**, W3C. **[fetch]**
<https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/visual-presentation.html>. A mechanism is to be available so that
blocks of text are no wider than 80 characters, 40 if CJK; are not justified; and have line spacing of at least
one and a half within paragraphs. The explanation cites studies in which CJK characters are about twice as wide as
others at equal readability.

**GB/T 9704-2012, 党政机关公文格式** (General Administration of Quality Supervision and the Standardization
Administration, issued 2012-06-29). **[fetch]**, from a university's repost:
<https://www.xcc.edu.cn/whcmxy/365743/365751/395860/index.html>. 5.2.2: 3 号 FangSong (16 pt) unless stated. 5.2.3:
normally 22 lines a page and 28 characters a line. 3.2: one "line" is the height of a character plus 7/8 of it, a
pitch of 1.875 em. 5.2.1: a text block of 156 mm × 225 mm on A4. It is a small format, which the owner excludes as a
model; it is given because it is the one Chinese standard found that fixes line length and pitch as numbers.

**GB 40070-2021** (near-sightedness prevention requirements for school supplies; mandatory from 1 March 2022), as
reported in press and official summaries: textbook body text not smaller than 3 号 in grades 1–2, 4 号 in grades
3–4, 小 4 号 (12 pt) from grade 5 through senior high. **[snippet]** Print, at reading distance.

**Vendors.**

- Apple, Human Interface Guidelines, "Accessibility" (change log to 9 June 2025): default and minimum sizes for
  custom type are 29 pt and 23 pt on tvOS, 17 and 11 on iOS and iPadOS, 13 and 10 on macOS. **[fetch]**
  <https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility.md>. Nothing on that page is specific
  to Chinese. If the tvOS canvas is 1080 pt high (from memory, not checked), 29 and 23 pt are 2.69 % and 2.13 % of
  the height: 24.2 and 19.2 pt on the 900 pt page.
- Google, Material Design: Chinese, Japanese and Korean are "dense" scripts; the first version of the guideline is
  reported to add 0.1 em of line height for them, and later versions to say only that line height is slightly
  larger than for Latin. **[snippet]**; the pages need JavaScript, **[not reached]**.
- Adobe: not searched in the time allowed.

## What was compared

The study sets its sources side by side and weighs none of them.

- Justification: clreq says Chinese body text should be justified; WCAG 1.4.8 asks that text can be shown not
  justified.
- Line length for Chinese: at most about 40 characters (JLReq for horizontal setting; WCAG for CJK); 28 characters
  (GB/T 9704, A4). Line pitch: 1.5–2.0 em (JLReq), 1.875 em (GB/T 9704), at least 1.5 (WCAG).
- Words on a projected page: at most 30 (IEDM), a median of 40 found in the wild (Tufte), 21 against 41.5 in the
  one experiment that reports it (Garner and Alley), 150–200 for a page meant to be read and not projected (Duarte,
  reported).

| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

| Much text read in silence                | A company's account of its practice; counted densities of books and news pages                          | Read on paper or at a desk; no measurement; nothing found for a room reading a dense page in silence |
| A dense page read from a projection      | One study: ten minutes of proofreading projected Chinese text, better at 24′ and 48′ than at 16′        | One study of thirty young adults; ten minutes of proofreading                                        |

## What follows, and where it applies

Nothing here is in the product; see the [committee's record](edition-forms-committee-2026-11-15.md).

- **Ruling R2** makes a presentation page one claim and one exhibit, and **ruling R3** a declared capacity per page
  class. R3's first trial value was withdrawn on 2026-11-16: the capacity is six lines of the statement tier at scale
  B, the scale of the page the owner preferred of the two shown, a two-line statement counting as two and a table's
  rows counting as lines. The dissent to the draft notes that the support for assertion and evidence is for narrated
  talks and does not establish silent reading.
- The second proof measured what a statement page holds at the trial scales; see
  [what the second proof showed](second-proof.md).
- The figures for line length and pitch bear on statement pages and on the booklet; the page on
  [layout of pages that are mostly text](text-page-layout.md) carries the Latin ranges beside them.

It applies to pages shown to a group. The figures for print (GB/T 9704, the Japanese requirements, GB 40070) describe
pages read at reading distance and are given for their numbers, not as models.
